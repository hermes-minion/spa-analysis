Verdict

Neutral. Central value C$1.60 (price C$1.62, −1.5%). Bear C$0.00 · Base C$1.10 · Bull C$7.70, probabilities 45/40/15.

The equity is not a claim on earnings; it is a 2.2-year call option on enterprise value, struck at 6.25x EBITDA. At C$1.62 the market capitalisation is C$108.8M against C$1,351M of enterprise value — the shares are 8.0% of the capital structure. Every turn of EBITDA multiple is worth roughly C$3 per share, and 0.5x of multiple compression is worth the entire market cap. The option is roughly fairly priced today: the 6.8x the market pays is 0.55x above the level at which shareholders are wiped out.

What the option needs to pay off is not heroics, but it is not in the current numbers either: EBITDA back above C$215M (from C$198.8M) and a re-rating to 8x, or a sale at that level. What kills it is a multiple of 5.5x or a covenant amendment that pulls the lenders into the room.

Executive summary

  1. The balance sheet is the company. C$1,283.8M of debt and converts sit above C$198.8M of EBITDA. Cash interest of C$117.3M consumes 59% of EBITDA and 76% of operating cash flow. Interest cover is 1.69x; after capex it is 1.52x.
  2. Deleveraging in FY26 was an asset sale, not cash generation. Net debt fell C$116.4M; the Credas disposal contributed C$142.1M net. Operations, after interest, capex, leases and C$35.5M of legacy acquisition holdbacks, were C$26.7M cash-negative. The tie is exact to within C$1.0M (§6).
  3. The covenant is no longer theoretical. The 5.80x first-lien test only applies when revolver drawings exceed 35% of the C$105M facility (C$36.75M). At 30 June drawings were C$29.5M (28.1%); after year-end the company drew a further C$31.0M, taking drawings to C$60.5M (57.6%). The covenant is live at the 30 September 2026 test date — the quarter reported in tomorrow's print — and the company says it is holding the cash.
  4. Covenant headroom is thinner than the 5.17x headline implies. Back-solving the ratio gives a covenant EBITDA of C$220.1M — 10.8% above reported adjusted EBITDA, consistent with the C$19.9M of run-rate cost savings the credit agreement allows as an add-back. A 10.9% fall in covenant EBITDA breaches 5.80x. The business fell 14.6% last year.
  5. The accounting is not yet clean. FY26 carries a material weakness in internal controls (disclosed with the Q4 print), a C$32.5M restatement of Q3 FY26 net income booked through Q4, a clean but going-concern-flagged audit (the auditor named "preparation on a going concern basis" as a key audit matter), and a C$1,061.5M goodwill balance that was tested twice because book value exceeded market cap. The auditor's opinion is unmodified; the process around it is not.

2. Business model, revenue mix and seasonality

Dye & Durham sells workflow software and transaction data to law firms and lenders. Two product lines, four reported geographies [F]:

  • Legal Software. Legal practice management (matter management, accounting, document automation) and Data Insights & Due Diligence (corporate searches, land and property reports, entity management, due-diligence reports). This is the sticky, subscription-heavy core.
  • Financial Technology. Payments infrastructure (CANACT BillPay), digital mortgage processing, settlement and registry services for roughly 100 financial institutions in Canada and Australia. Infrastructure-like, contract-based, lower growth, high margin.

Geographic mix (FY26 revenue): Canada 58%, UK & Ireland 23%, Australia 17%, South Africa 2% [F].

The revenue is not one business with one risk. Conveyancing and search revenue is transactional and moves with Canadian real-estate volumes; practice management is recurring seats; financial technology is contracted with the large banks. The company last disclosed contracted annual recurring revenue (ARR) at C$136.7M as of Q4 FY2024 — about 30% of that year's revenue — and has not updated it in the two years since, through a period in which revenue fell 9% [F, AIF; absence of an FY25/FY26 figure verified across the FY26 MD&A and AIF]. Management describes the majority of revenue as earned "on a highly recurring transactional-fee basis", which is a definitional sleight of hand: a fee that recurs because the transaction recurs is not the same as a subscription. The practical consequence is that an investor cannot underwrite the "sticky franchise" claim with a disclosed retention metric — only with the segment revenue trend, which is negative in every region, and an ARR disclosure that stopped when the growth did.

Seasonality is real and is the timing risk for the covenant [F, MD&A "Summary of Quarterly Results"]:

Quarter (fiscal) Calendar Revenue pattern Adj. EBITDA margin FY26 FY25

The company states plainly that conveyancing peaks in Q4 and Q1 and slows in Q2 and Q3. Two consequences: (i) the September and June quarters are when EBITDA — and therefore covenant headroom — is strongest; (ii) the March quarter is when a weak year shows up first. The margin path (58% → 56% → 51% → 45% in FY25; 47% → 47% → 47% → 53% in FY26) shows a business that lost 11 points of margin year-on-year in Q1 and regained 7.5 in Q4. That is a cost story, not yet a revenue story.

The dates are not what they look like. If the 2029 notes are not paid, extended, refinanced or replaced before maturity, the Term Loan B and the revolver each accelerate to 91 days before 15 April 2029 — approximately 14 January 2029 [F, Note 12(a)]. The "2031 term loan" is therefore a 2029 obligation in disguise. Working backwards: the converts mature 1 November 2028, the acceleration date is mid-January 2029, and the notes mature 15 April 2029. The company has 26 months to refinance or sell, and its free cash flow after interest is roughly C$9M a year.

Priority. The notes, the TLB and the revolver share a first-ranking general security agreement over all present and after-acquired property. The converts are senior unsecured. There is no second-lien tranche and no off-balance-sheet financing [F].

Measure C is the economically correct one for a solvent outcome: the converts are carried at C$104.4M, but the contractual claim is C$160.4M. If the company pays them in cash or refinances them at par, shareholders fund the difference; if they convert, shareholders fund it in dilution. The C$56.0M gap is C$0.83 per share — 51% of the current share price. Every per-share number in §10 uses measure A (consistent with the market convention and the brief), and the memo grid shows B.

The converts are the real dilution risk, and the brief does not carry it. At a C$1.62 conversion price the C$160.4M face would issue 99.0M new shares — +147%, taking the count to 166.2M. At C$2.00 it is +119%; at C$3.00, +80%. The strike is C$40.00, so this is not voluntary conversion — it is a negotiated exchange or a discounted refinancing in November 2028, both of which transfer value from common shareholders. The company's own model marks the converts at 65 cents on the dollar, which is a credit opinion embedded in its own accounts.

Reporting covenants were breached in form, waived in fact. FY26 began with the company late on its FY25 audited statements and its Q1 FY26 interim statements. It obtained waivers under the Senior Credit Agreement, paid consent fees to lenders, and paid an extra 25bp on the 2029 notes until the statements were delivered [F]. That episode is why the lenders now have advisers.

5. Liquidity and covenant headroom: the test that just switched on

The covenant is a springing one, and it has sprung. The Senior Credit Agreement requires a consolidated first-lien net leverage ratio no higher than 5.80x at each fiscal quarter-end — but only if revolver drawings have exceeded the "Specified Threshold", which the financial statements define as 35% of the C$105.0M facility, or C$36.75M [F, Note 12(c)].

Date Revolver drawn % of facility Covenant tested?
30 Jun 2026 29.5 28.1% No (below threshold)
Post-year-end draw +31.0 — —
30 Sep 2026 60.5 [E] 57.6% Yes

The MD&A is explicit: the C$31.0M was drawn "given the timing of semi-annual payments on its debt", and the company "expects that the additional draw will be held in cash at the end of the three months ended September 30, 2026" [F]. In plain terms: the company borrowed on the revolver to make the October interest payment and is holding the proceeds so that net debt does not rise at the test date. That is a liquidity-management fact, not a growth decision, and it is the single most important thing to read in tomorrow's balance sheet.

Headroom is narrower than 5.17x versus 5.80x sounds. Back-solving the reported ratio gives the covenant EBITDA the lenders are actually using:

  • Net first-lien debt = 1,179.4 − 41.4 = C$1,138.0M
  • Reported ratio 5.17x → covenant EBITDA = C$220.1M
  • Reported adjusted EBITDA = C$198.8M → the covenant definition is 10.8% higher, consistent with the C$19.9M of run-rate cost savings added back (198.8 + 19.9 = 218.7)

Consequences:

  • Breach point: covenant EBITDA of C$196.2M (1,138.0 ÷ 5.80). That is roughly C$176M of reported adjusted EBITDA — an 11.3% decline from FY26.
  • Debt capacity: C$138.7M of additional net first-lien debt before 5.80x, assuming EBITDA holds.
  • Context: adjusted EBITDA fell 14.6% in FY26 and 7.9% in FY25. One more year at the FY26 rate breaches the covenant. One year at half that rate does not.

Against ~C$110M of annual cash interest, that is roughly one year of debt service with zero operating cash flow — and the revolver is now 58% drawn. A second C$31M draw to fund the April 2027 coupon would take utilisation to 87%, at which point the facility is effectively closed and the company is dependent on the sale process or an amendment.

Seasonality cuts both ways. Conveyancing peaks in the June and September quarters, so the 30 September test is the easiest of the year and the 31 March 2027 test is the hardest. A company that is compliant in Q1 and tight in Q3 is the normal pattern here — which is why one good September quarter (Q4 FY26 was June; Q1 FY27 is September) cannot be extrapolated.

What the lenders' adviser means. Bloomberg reported on 27 July 2026 that the lenders had retained Houlihan Lokey [U]. Advisers to a lender group are retained for one of three reasons: to negotiate an amendment (benign), to prepare for a liability-management exercise (dilutive), or to shop the debt (neutral for equity, bad for the multiple). The company has since disclosed a material weakness, missed consensus EPS by a wide margin, and drawn further on the revolver. None of that shortens the odds of the second outcome.

Three observations:

  1. Interest consumes 76% of operating cash flow. C$117.3M of C$153.4M. Interest cover on EBITDA is 1.69x; after capex it is 1.52x. For a business with a 48% EBITDA margin, that is a bond-like cash profile with equity volatility.
  2. Operating cash flow is flattered by working capital and low cash taxes. Receivables fell C$27.4M (from 88.1 to 71.0), contributing most of a C$10.7M net working-capital release; cash taxes were only C$7.7M because the group is carrying losses [F]. Neither repeats indefinitely.
  3. The holdbacks are not optional. C$30.1M remains on the balance sheet as current, and the FY26 payment was C$35.5M. These are contractual liabilities from the 2021–2024 acquisition programme, sitting in investing activities where they do not touch EBITDA or "operating" cash flow. They are the difference between a company that breaks even and one that burns.

The entire C$116.4M reduction is the Credas sale less the cash the business consumed on its legacy obligations. Operations contributed C$8.8M and then took back C$35.5M. Without the disposal, net debt would have risen by roughly C$26M.

Two supporting facts make the picture sharper:

  • The term-debt paydown was funded by the asset sale; the revolver was a swing line. Term debt and notes were repaid C$102.2M, of which C$99.8M came from the Credas proceeds. The revolver saw C$52.5M of draws and C$70.5M of repayments — a net C$18.0M reduction — so it was used for timing, not for deleveraging. The C$31.0M draw after year-end is the same pattern one quarter later [F].
  • The asset-sale prepayment was compulsory. The 2029 notes indenture required an excess-proceeds offer after the Credas disposal; the company ran it and announced the results on 12 March 2026 [C]. That is why the notes balance fell by C$62.2M rather than the cash being retained.

At 0.65% of enterprise value, free cash flow is not a valuation input — it is a constraint. It cannot repay C$1.28B of debt, and at C$9M a year it repays 0.7% of the principal annually. Deleveraging from here requires either another asset sale, an EBITDA recovery, or a refinancing that lowers the cost of debt. The first is finite (the FS division is the remaining candidate), the second is not in the current numbers, and the third depends on the same EBITDA the equity depends on.

The material weakness. The CEO and CFO concluded that internal control over financial reporting was not effective at 30 June 2026, and that the same weakness existed at 31 March 2026. The company describes remediation but does not quantify it [F, MD&A]. A material weakness is not a restatement; it is a statement that the process that produces the numbers cannot be relied on to catch errors of this size. It arrived in the same release as the C$4.8M discrepancy in the FY26 net loss described in §3 — which is exactly the kind of error a working control set is meant to catch.

The auditor's two key audit matters are the two things an equity holder should worry about [F]:

  1. Goodwill impairment. C$1,061.5M of goodwill, tested at 1 April 2026 with no impairment, then re-tested at 30 June 2026 because "the Company's book value of net assets exceeded its market capitalization". Discount rates 10.8%–18.8%, terminal growth 2.0%–3.0%. No impairment recorded.
  2. Preparation on a going concern basis. The auditor states that evaluating management's assessment "involved significant auditor judgement in understanding management's overall plans, specifically the key assumptions … in developing future cash flow forecasts and forecast of leverage levels to meet required debt covenants".

That second KAM is the balance-sheet risk stated in the auditor's own words. Management concluded there is no material uncertainty; the auditor did not dissent, but it did not treat the question as routine either.

The goodwill wedge

At 30 June 2026 the company's book equity was C$280.4M against a market capitalisation of roughly C$93M. That is an impairment trigger, and the test passed only because value-in-use — a management DCF at a 10.8%–18.8% discount rate — produced recoverable amounts above carrying values. Aggregate carrying value of the CGUs is roughly C$1,596M [E: goodwill + intangibles + PP&E + right-of-use + net working capital], which implies the DCF is valuing the enterprise at at least 8.0x FY26 EBITDA. The market is at 6.8x.

That gap is the whole argument in one number: management's own model says the business is worth at least 15% more than the market pays, and the market says management's model is too generous. One of them will be marked to reality — the next annual test is 1 April 2027, and the Q4 FY27 accounts will force the comparison again.

What "adjusted" means here

FY26 Adjusted EBITDA of C$198.8M is struck after adding back C$49.7M of "acquisition, restructuring and other costs" — 25% of the reported figure. The composition, per Note 18: professional fees and integration costs, filing and waiver costs, severance and executive separation costs, fair-value movements on contingent consideration (C$1.4M, the only clearly non-cash item), and legal and advisory fees associated with board changes and the delayed filings [F].

Almost all of that is cash, and almost all of it has recurred every year since 2021. Two consequences:

  • The C$8.8M of free cash flow in §6 already bears these costs. That is why the cash number and the EBITDA number tell different stories: the EBITDA line excludes what the bank account pays.
  • If you charge the business even C$20–25M of normalised public-company and restructuring costs, EBITDA is C$174–179M and the EV/EBITDA at C$1.62 rises to 7.5–7.8x — above the multiple a strategic buyer is likely to pay for a shrinking asset.

Adjusted EBITDA is a legitimate measure of the operating business. It is not a measure of what shareholders own.

The moat is real but narrower than the bull case implies:

  • Data insights and conveyancing search are less sticky and more price-sensitive. Searches are largely a pass-through of public-record data with a workflow wrapper.   • The pricing model has already been retreated from. That is the right commercial decision and a permanent hit to revenue per user — the growth that the 2021–2023 model assumed is not coming back.

  • The Competition Bureau inquiry into the company's position in the conveyancing software market (court order for records, November 2024) has been discontinued; the Commissioner confirmed no further investigatory steps are contemplated [U]. A tail risk that was in the 2024–25 bear case is gone.
  • The Ontario government awarded the company a four-year contract for electronic business registry services in March 2026 [C]. That re-bases a chunk of the data-insights franchise and is the single most concrete commercial win of the year.

What the standstill actually does

The 5 November agreements are the most consequential governance event of the year, and they cut both ways:

  • For the bull case: 36% of the register is now contractually locked into supporting the board and any transaction through September 2027. A sale needs a majority of the minority in practice; with 36% committed, the approval hurdle is materially lower. The litigation that consumed board time is dismissed. EdgePoint — a long-only value manager — has a board seat and a stated interest in "operating performance and balance sheet discipline."
  • For the bear case: a standstill prevents Plantro from making or increasing a bid. The most plausible natural buyer of this company — the founder who tried in 2025 — is now inside the tent and contractually barred from bidding until September 2027. If anyone hoped for a Proud rescue premium, the agreement removes it.

The sale process is alive on paper and stale in fact

The MD&A dated 28 September says the process "is ongoing", overseen by a Strategic Committee of the board [F]. On the Q4 call, six weeks ago, the interim CEO said the review had been delayed by management changes but was "close to completion" [U]. Nothing has been announced since. A process launched on 29 December 2025 that has produced no transaction, no stalking-horse and no update for eleven months is not a catalyst; it is a background condition. The rights plan and the voting support make a deal easier to execute, but they do not create a buyer.

Three readings are consistent with the evidence: (i) a sale of the Canadian FS division is being negotiated and will land; (ii) the board has priced the company above what bidders will pay and is waiting for EBITDA to recover; (iii) the lenders' adviser is now the fulcrum and any transaction will be a recapitalisation rather than a sale. The company's behaviour — drawing the revolver to pay interest rather than selling an asset — is more consistent with (ii) or (iii) than with (i).

The people problem

The company has had, in twelve months: five board chairs [U], two CEOs plus a board committee running the office of the CEO (Tsivin, the Transformation Committee, Schulte), two interim CFOs (Bell, Waszak), and a board that now includes the founder, his holding company's nominee, and an activist-nominated director. The interim CEO is a career software operating executive and the interim CFO is managing cash conservatively [U] — both are credible caretakers, neither is a permanent answer. The permanent CEO search is the single cheapest positive catalyst available to the equity, and it has now run for eight months.

Alignment and conflicts

Two structural features deserve flagging:

  • The Investor Rights Agreement gives Plantro and OneMove nomination rights, pre-emptive rights and demand registration rights, and entitles Plantro's nominee to serve as Chair (with a carve-out preventing Matthew Proud from chairing while he is CEO) [F, AIF]. That is an unusual concentration of board-level influence for a holder that is also a potential counterparty.
  • The board is running a sale process that could sell the Canadian FS division to a financial buyer while the founder's holding company sits on the board with a standstill. There is no evidence of impropriety; there is a clear requirement for an independent committee, and the existence of a Strategic Committee is the mitigation.

Governance verdict: improving at the margin, still a discount item. The standstill and the litigation settlement are genuine progress. Five chairs, two CEOs plus a board committee, and an interim finance function in one year are not.

10. Valuation: an option, priced about right

Why not a DCF

A discounted cash flow on this company would produce a number and no information. Free cash flow after interest is C$8.8M — 0.65% of enterprise value — and is negative after legacy obligations. The value of the equity is not the present value of that stream; it is the residual of enterprise value over a fixed claim. So the right instrument is a multiple on EBITDA, applied to the whole enterprise, with the debt subtracted.

11. The bull case, in its strongest form

1. The operating leverage is now visible in the numbers, not just in the deck. Q4 revenue +4% ex-Credas produced EBITDA +18% and a 53% margin, with operating costs down C$8.4M year on year. Because the cost base is largely fixed, the incremental margin on recovered revenue is close to 90%. A return to FY25 revenue (C$440M) on the FY26 cost base would produce roughly C$225M of EBITDA — 13% above today — without a single new customer. The company has already delivered C$19.9M of annualised savings against an C$11.0M target, and has identified another C$6–8M for FY27.

2. Canada is the engine and it has turned. Canada grew 8.7% in Q4 with segment EBITDA +13.3%, driven by practice management and payment infrastructure. Canada is 58% of revenue and its end market — Canadian residential transactions — is at a multi-decade low. Any normalisation of housing volumes drops through at very high incremental margins.

3. The balance sheet has already improved materially, and the maturity profile is better than the headline. Total debt fell from C$1,587M to C$1,284M in twelve months. The Term Loan B has no further principal amortisation before 2031, because the Credas proceeds prepaid the remaining instalments. The 3.75% converts are gone. Cash interest fell from C$122.4M to C$117.3M in a year of rising rates.

4. Governance risk has collapsed. 36% of the register is contractually locked into supporting the board until September 2027; the litigation is dismissed; the board now has a long-only value investor (EdgePoint) and an operator with public-company CEO experience (Dennis, as observer). A sale, if agreed, is now approvable. The "founder versus company" discount that has hung over the stock for two years should compress.

5. The option is cheap in the only unit that matters. The equity is 8% of the capital structure and sits 0.55x of EBITDA above the wipeout line. Each turn of multiple is worth C$2.96 per share — 1.8x the current price. A 100bp reduction in the cost of debt, with no operational change at all, is worth C$1.23 per share.

6. The assets are strategic. Roughly 100 financial-institution relationships, the Ontario electronic business registry contract, and mission-critical workflow software with high switching costs. A strategic buyer with synergies can pay 8x and still earn its cost of capital; the Canadian FS division alone is the kind of asset a bank or payments processor would buy.

12. The bear case, in its strongest form

1. The arithmetic is unforgiving. Breakeven is 6.25x EBITDA; the stock trades at 6.80x. A 5.5x multiple — normal for a shrinking, levered software company with a material weakness — makes the equity worthless at any EBITDA in the plausible range. The market is not paying for the business; it is paying half a turn of optimism.

2. The business does not generate enough cash to service its own debt, let alone repay it. FCF after interest, capex and leases was C$8.8M; after the C$35.5M of legacy acquisition holdbacks it was negative C$26.7M. Interest consumes 76% of operating cash flow, cover is 1.69x, and the company drew C$31M on the revolver to fund the October coupon. That is the behaviour of a company managing liquidity, not a company deleveraging. FY26's C$116M reduction in net debt came entirely from selling Credas.

3. Every region is shrinking and the growth was bought with price. FY26 revenue −6% ex-Credas; Canada −5.4%, UK −13.2%, Australia −0.9%, South Africa −15.2%. Management attributes the decline to lost customers and contract renewals in practice management and data insights — the price increases of 2022–24 cost volume. The Q4 improvement is one quarter off a trough, in the seasonally strongest quarter of the year.

4. The numbers are not yet trustworthy. A material weakness in internal control at both 31 March and 30 June 2026; a C$24.6M restatement of Q3 net income; two different FY26 net losses (C$38.5M and C$43.3M) in the same press release; and an auditor who made going-concern assessment and goodwill impairment the two key audit matters. A buyer, a lender and a convert holder will all price that.

5. Governance is better, not good. Five chairs in under a year, an interim CEO and an interim CFO, the founder whose acquisition programme created the leverage back on the board, and a standstill that prevents the most plausible buyer from bidding until September 2027. The sale process has run eleven months and produced nothing.

6. The covenant is live with ~11% of EBITDA headroom, and the convert wall is behind it. Reported adjusted EBITDA can fall to roughly C$176M before 5.80x is breached — one more year at the FY26 rate of decline. A breach hands the lenders the initiative: an amendment at a price, or a liability-management exercise in which the C$160.4M of converts (marked at 65 cents by the company itself) and possibly part of the senior debt convert into equity ahead of the common.

7. The end markets do not help. Canadian housing volumes remain subdued, the UK is contracting, Australia is flat, and refinancing is expensive — the US 10-year is at 4.84% [M]. Nothing in the macro picture accelerates the deleveraging.

Where the two cases actually disagree

They agree the asset is good and the margins are structurally high. They disagree on exactly two things:

  1. What multiple a shrinking, levered, low-FCF software company deserves — 5.5x or 8.0x. That single judgement moves the equity from C$0 to C$7.
  2. Whether a transaction happens — a sale, a division sale, or a refinancing. The operational difference between C$190M and C$215M of EBITDA at a constant 6.8x is worth C$2.53 per share; the difference between 6.25x and 8.0x on the same C$199M of EBITDA is worth C$5.18.

The debate is not about the business. It is about the price of the business and the willingness of a lender or a buyer to set it.

14. Signposts: what confirms and what kills each case

Bull case — confirmations required

  1. Two consecutive quarters of positive organic revenue growth (Q1 and Q2 FY27), not one.
  2. Adjusted EBITDA margin ≥48% in Q1 and ≥50% in Q4 FY27, with costs flat or lower in absolute terms.
  3. At least C$15M of FCF after interest in each of the two peak quarters (Q1 and Q4), and no new revolver draw beyond the C$31M already taken.
  4. First-lien leverage ≤5.0x by Q4 FY27 — requires EBITDA ≥C$228M or C$100M+ of debt reduction.
  5. A named permanent CEO with software operating experience, before the FY27 year-end.
  6. A transaction: either a signed sale of the Canadian FS division at ≥8x, or a refinancing of the 2029 notes extending the wall beyond 2031 at ≤9.5%.
  7. The remaining C$6–8M of cost savings delivered on schedule, with no offsetting reinvestment above the line.

Bear case — confirmations required

  1. Revenue below C$100M in Q1 or Q2 FY27 (i.e., a return to decline after one good quarter).
  2. Margin below 44% in a peak quarter, which would mean the Q4 cost savings were seasonal or one-off.
  3. Any covenant amendment, waiver or forbearance, or first-lien leverage ≥5.5x.
  4. A third restatement or a further material-weakness disclosure; any auditor language on going concern.
  5. The converts exchanged at a discount to par (a debt-for-equity swap), which transfers value from common.
  6. No permanent CEO and no transaction update by the Q2 FY27 print (February 2027).
  7. A further revolver draw above C$70M (66% utilisation) before the April 2027 coupon.

Kill conditions (thesis dead, exit regardless of price)

  • A covenant breach that is not cured within the grace period.
  • Any restructuring announcement that equitises the converts or the senior debt without a fulsome common-shareholder recovery.
  • Loss of the Ontario registry contract or a major bank relationship.
  • A going-concern modification in the audit opinion.

Why neutral and not a buy, given a 4.8x bull case: the expected value sits at the price, the distribution is bimodal, and the probability of a total loss is roughly three times the probability of the good outcome. A 4.8x tail with a 45% chance of zero and no expected-value edge is not a risk premium; it is a lottery ticket priced at fair value. The market has done its job here.

Why not a sell, given a 6.25x wipeout line: the company has 26 months before the acceleration date, C$117M of liquidity, a lender group with an adviser (which implies negotiation, not liquidation), a live sale process, and an asset base that a strategic buyer values above the public market. A short at C$1.62 risks being run over by a single announcement.

If you own it: hold, size it as a binary (≤1% of a portfolio), and review at the Q1 print against the table in §13. The stock has already tripled off the September low; the easy money was made by buying the panic, not by buying the price.

If you do not own it: the entry zone is C$1.00–1.10 — the level at which you are paying ~6.6x, roughly a third of a turn above the wipeout line, and the probability-weighted value is 45–60% above your cost. Above C$1.40 you are paying the market's own estimate of fair value for a governance-discounted, covenant-constrained, cash-negative business.

Would I buy it with my own money at C$1.62? No. At C$1.05, as a small option with a two-year horizon, yes.

18. Method, limitations and how to update

Method. Enterprise value = market capitalisation + net debt; equity value = EV − net debt. Multiples are applied to Adjusted EBITDA as reported by the company, with a memo grid on a fully-loaded net debt. The DCF is deliberately not used: free cash flow after interest is C$8.8M and negative after legacy obligations, so a DCF would be a present-value calculation dominated by assumptions about a terminal value 10 years after the debt matures. The optionality is in the capital structure, so the model prices the capital structure.

Bases fixed once, used everywhere: price C$1.62 (TSX close, 6 November 2026, Yahoo Finance); shares 67.18M (FY26 weighted average basic; the count has not moved materially in two years); balance date 30 June 2026 (audited); all figures in Canadian dollars, no FX conversion of the USD-denominated debt (carrying values are used as presented).

Model. model_DND.py in this folder reproduces every table in §4–§10 from hard-coded filing inputs and prints to model_output.txt. To update after tomorrow's print: replace OCF, cash interest, capex, leases, holdbacks, debt and cash with the Q1 figures; re-run; the sensitivity grid, scenario table and covenant back-solve refresh automatically. The covenant back-solve (covenant EBITDA = net first-lien debt ÷ reported ratio) is the first thing to re-check.

Limitations and unverified items.

  • The Q1 FY27 results date (10 November 2026) comes from the vendor calendar; no company notice was located [M].
  • Individual ownership percentages for Plantro and EdgePoint are not disclosed in the sources reviewed; only the ~36% combined figure from the 5 November release is verified [C].
  • The exact date of the Competition Bureau discontinuance was not located; the substance (inquiry discontinued, no further steps) is from a TheFly summary of the company's announcement [U].
  • The converts' fair value (C$104.4M) is a Level 3 model output reflecting the company's own credit-risk assumption, not a traded price [F, Note 14/Note 20]. It is used as a market signal, not as a mark.
  • The Canadian FS division EBITDA (~C$50M) used in the divestiture illustration is an estimate; the company reports geography, not the FS/non-FS split [E].
  • Scenario probabilities are subjective [E]. They are stated so the reader can substitute their own and re-derive the central value.
  • The adjusted EPS miss quoted in market coverage (a loss of C$0.66 versus a forecast near breakeven) is a vendor-defined measure; the audited net loss per share is C$0.57. The difference is finance-cost and FX marks, not cash [M][F].
