Oklo Inc. (NYSE: OKLO) — Equity Research Note

Price: $23.92 (NYSE close, 13 Nov 2026) · Market cap: $4.67B · EV: $2.17B · Revenue TTM: $3.1M · FCF: -$452M · Net cash: $2.50B Verdict: Neutral (High risk) · 12-month probability-weighted value: ~$26 (+9%) Screening note; data as of 13-Nov-2026. Research, not investment advice.

1. The setup

  • The best-funded pre-revenue nuclear name — and still a venture bet. Oklo holds $2.50B of net cash against a $4.67B market cap, so the enterprise value is only $2.17B. The company has no meaningful revenue ($3.1M TTM) and burns ~$452M a year, giving it roughly 5.5 years of runway.
  • The stock has collapsed 69% YTD and 86% from its high, as the entire nuclear/AI-power complex de-rated. The shares are far below the 50-DMA ($32.1) and 200-DMA ($50.6); short interest is 19.4% of shares out.
  • The asset is real: the Aurora fast reactor (15-75 MWe), a DOE site at Idaho National Laboratory, an NRC combined-license application, and a customer pipeline that includes data-center and industrial offtakers. Sam Altman is chairman; that has been both a credibility booster and a headline risk.
  • The business model is build-own-operate-plus-fuel: Oklo intends to sell power through long-term PPAs and recycle fuel, rather than just sell reactors. That is potentially a higher-value model, but it requires capital and regulatory approvals Oklo does not yet have.
  • The balance sheet is the moat for now: $2.5B cash, $4.4M debt, 5+ years of runway. That is a genuine advantage versus NuScale (~18 months) and gives Oklo room to reach first power.
  • The valuation is an option price: EV $2.17B for a company with no revenue, no operating license and a first deployment targeted for 2027-28. The equity is a call on the Aurora program, not a cash-flow story.
  • Momentum/sentiment: 19% short interest, beta 1.3, and a stock that trades on nuclear headlines. Expect continued volatility.

2. Business and moat

Oklo designs small fast reactors (liquid-metal-cooled) and plans to own/operate them, selling electricity under long-term contracts. It also intends to build a fuel-recycling capability (using DOE material) — a potential cost advantage and a political lightning rod.

The moat is regulatory/execution: an NRC license for a fast reactor would be a first-of-a-kind asset, and the DOE site access is scarce. The risks are that the NRC schedule slips, costs overrun, or the offtake contracts are not bankable.
